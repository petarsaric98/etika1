Lesson 6

The Golden Mean

So far we have asked what to do. Aristotle asks something else: what kind of person to become? His answer fits in one sentence — and takes a lifetime to learn.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: explain the difference between asking “what should I do?” and “what should I be?”; define virtue and explain why it is a habit; define the golden mean and name both vices for a given virtue; explain what it means that the mean is “relative to us”; explain why not every action has a mean; and explain the concept of eudaimonia.

1. Two different questions

So far we have watched people at the moment of decision: Antigone before Creon, Oedipus before the oracle, Midas before Dionysus, Icarus under the sun. Each time the question was the same: what is the right thing to do in this situation?

But there is another way of thinking about ethics. Notice that in hard moments some people behave decently almost without thinking, while others fall apart — and this has little to do with how many rules they have learned. So Aristotle asks differently: what kind of person must you be for right action to come out of you by itself?

Ethics of acts

Asks: what should I do? Looks for a rule that fits the situation. It is good when a case has to be judged.

Virtue ethics

Asks: what should I be? Looks for traits of character acquired by habit. It is good when you have to raise a person, not settle one case.

Who was Aristotle?

Aristotle (384–322 BC) spent twenty years as a student of Plato, and Plato had been a student of Socrates — three generations of teacher and pupil on which the whole of Western philosophy rests. Later he tutored the young Alexander the Great, and in Athens he founded his own school, the Lyceum. His most important book on ethics is called the Nicomachean Ethics, after his son Nicomachus — or his father, since both had that name.

2. Virtue is a habit

Definition

Virtue (Greek arete)

Virtue is an acquired, lasting trait of character that makes a person good and makes them act well: courage, justice, honesty, generosity, gentleness.

The crucial point is that virtue is neither a feeling nor knowledge, but a habit. Nobody is born courageous, and reading a book about courage makes nobody courageous.

So how do you become good? Aristotle's answer sounds almost too simple: by doing good, again and again.

Builders become builders by building, and lyre players by playing the lyre. In the same way we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts.
Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II (paraphrase)
Aristotle answers Socrates

In the fourth lesson Socrates claimed that evil is ignorance: whoever knows what is good will do it. Aristotle says that isn't enough. We all know we should study, that we shouldn't shout at people we love, that we should tell the truth — and we all sometimes fail to do it. Knowledge is only the beginning. Between knowing and doing stands character, and character is trained like a muscle.

3. The golden mean

Definition

The golden mean (mesotes)

Every virtue stands between two vices: one in which there is too much of something, and one in which there is too little of it. Courage is not the opposite of cowardice but the mean between cowardice and reckless boldness.

Daedalus' “neither too high nor too low” from the last lesson is the same thought in a single sentence.

Two common misreadings of “the mean”

1. The mean is not mathematical. It is not “halfway” and not the same for everyone. A moderate amount of food for one person is a lot, for an athlete it is little. Aristotle says the mean is relative to us: at the right time, towards the right person, in the right way. That is why virtue requires practical wisdom, not a calculator — and why it was not enough for Icarus to know the rule by heart.

2. Not every action has a mean. There is no “moderate amount” of betrayal, malice or murder. Some things are wrong in every quantity. The mean applies to feelings and dispositions, not to all deeds. This is also the most common objection raised against Aristotle.

Practical advice

How do you hit the mean?

Aristotle admits the mean is hard to hit, like the centre of a circle. So he gives two pieces of advice:

1. Steer clear of the worse extreme. The two vices are not equally dangerous. If you don't know where the mean is, stay closer to the less harmful side.

2. Know your own tendency and pull towards the opposite side. If you know you lean towards irritability, aim slightly towards indifference — “the way you straighten a bent stick”: you bend it the other way so that it ends up straight. For that you first have to know yourself.

4. Exercise: find the mean

For each behaviour, decide whether there is too little of something, whether it hits the mean, or whether there is too much.

How does it look?

A friend shows you a new haircut that you honestly think doesn't suit them, and asks what you think. They have an important event tomorrow.

5. What is it all for?

Concept

Eudaimonia

Aristotle says we do all this for the sake of eudaimonia. The word is usually translated as “happiness”, but that is a poor translation. It does not mean a good mood, but a life that has succeeded as a whole, the life of someone who has realised the best in themselves. So nobody can be judged by a single day or a single success. People have argued about eudaimonia for centuries — not about whether we all want it, but about what it is.

One swallow does not make a spring, nor does one day; and so one act does not make a person good.
Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics (paraphrase)

Final tasks

Pick one

1. Your own table. Write down three virtues you lack and find both vices for each (too little and too much). For each one, mark which vice you yourself lean towards.

2. One swallow. Choose one virtue and one small, concrete act that shows it. Repeat it for seven days and write one sentence each day. At the end, answer: was day seven easier than day one?

3. Where there is no mean. Name three actions for which there is no “right amount”, and explain how they differ from those that have one.
