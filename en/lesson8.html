Lesson 8

Trust

Opening the door to a stranger. Betraying those who believed in you. Not turning round when every part of you says turn round. Three myths about what trust is worth.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the three myths; explain the Greek idea of xenia (hospitality); explain why Tantalus's crime is a betrayal of trust; and use Orpheus to explain that trust is a decision, not a feeling.

1. Without trust there is nothing

When you buy a sandwich, you trust it isn't poisoned. When you lend your notes, you trust you'll get them back. Trust is invisible until it breaks. The Greeks knew that one of the worst crimes is to betray someone who trusted you.

2. Philemon and Baucis: a door open to a stranger

🏠
Hospitality

A thousand closed doors

Zeus and Hermes disguised themselves as tired travellers and went round a village in Phrygia. They knocked on a thousand doors. Every one stayed shut.

One hut

Only an old man, Philemon, and his wife Baucis, the poorest people in the village, let them in. They gave everything they had: a few vegetables, a bit of bacon, cheap wine. Halfway through dinner they noticed that the wine jug kept refilling itself. Realising who their guests were, they tried to kill their only goose for them. The goose fled into the gods' laps, and Zeus told them to leave it be.

The reward

The gods flooded the valley and the houses of those who had turned them away. The hut became a temple. The old couple could make one wish, and they asked for just two things: to serve in that temple and to die at the same moment, so neither would have to bury the other. When their time came, they turned into an oak and a linden growing from one trunk. The story comes from Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Concept

Xenia

The sacred rule of hospitality. A stranger who knocks must be given food, a place to sleep and protection before you ask his name. Its guardian was Zeus himself, as Zeus Xenios, protector of guests. That is why the Greeks said: you never know who is knocking at your door.

Why the poorest?

The rich had something to lose, so they saw the stranger as a threat. Philemon and Baucis had almost nothing, yet gave it all. The myth doesn't reward the amount given, but the willingness to trust someone unknown.

Your door

A new student from another town joins your class. He knows nobody and sits alone at break.

3. Tantalus: the guest who betrayed his hosts

🍇
Betrayal of trust

At the table of the gods

Tantalus was a son of Zeus and a wealthy king. The gods liked him so much that he was the only mortal invited to their feasts on Olympus. No human had ever been such a guest.

Three betrayals

Tantalus abused that trust. He stole nectar and ambrosia, the food of the gods, and took it to humans. He gave away secrets he heard at the table. And then he did something terrible: to test whether the gods really knew everything, he served them his own son Pelops for dinner. The gods recognised at once what was in front of them and brought Pelops back to life.

The punishment

In Tartarus, Tantalus stands in a pool up to his chin, with a branch full of fruit above him. When he bends to drink, the water drains away. When he reaches for the fruit, the wind lifts the branch. He is forever hungry and thirsty, with food and water always within reach.

Words that stayed

The English verb to tantalise comes from this myth: to tease with something you can't have. The chemical element tantalum was also named after him, because it doesn't "drink" acid: it won't dissolve even when it's in it up to the neck.

Look at how the punishment matches the crime. Tantalus was given everything and still took more. His punishment lets him see everything and have nothing. The Greeks believed a punishment should fit the crime.

4. Orpheus and Eurydice: don't look back

🎵
Trust as a decision

A song in the underworld

Orpheus was the greatest singer of all time. When he played, animals followed him and stones moved. On their wedding day his wife Eurydice was bitten by a snake and died. Orpheus went down into the underworld alive and sang before Hades and Persephone until even they wept.

One condition

Hades agreed: Eurydice may follow him back to the world of the living. There was one condition. Orpheus walks in front and must not look back until both of them are out in the light.

At the very exit

They climbed for a long time, in silence. Orpheus couldn't hear her footsteps. Had Hades tricked him? Was she even there? At the very exit he couldn't bear it and turned round. He saw her once more, for an instant, and then she vanished back into the dark, this time for ever.

What is trust?

Orpheus knew the condition and wanted to succeed. But trust isn't knowledge. It begins exactly where you can't check. If he could have heard her footsteps, he wouldn't have needed trust. He needed it precisely because he couldn't. Trust is the decision not to check.

Your looking back

Your best friend says he lost his phone, which is why he hasn't answered all weekend. Someone tells you they saw him on his phone in a café.

Philemon trusted a stranger and gained everything. The gods trusted Tantalus and he lost everything. Orpheus only had to trust for a few more steps.
