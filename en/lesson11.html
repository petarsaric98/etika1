Lesson 11

Revenge or Justice

A son whom the gods order to avenge his father, and who is then hunted for that very revenge. An old king who walks alone at night into the tent of the man who killed his son. Two myths about how the cycle of revenge is broken: once by a court, once by tears.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the myth of Orestes and the myth of Priam visiting Achilles; name at least three differences between revenge and punishment; explain the principles of proportionality and impartiality; explain what forgiveness is and what it is not; and compare two ways of breaking the cycle of revenge: that of an institution and that of an individual.

1. A cycle that never stops by itself

When someone does us wrong, the first thing we often feel is the urge to pay them back. That feeling is as old as humanity and it is not entirely wrong: it tells us an injustice has happened. The problem is what comes next. The person we paid back rarely thinks it was fair, so they pay us back in turn. This is how a cycle of revenge begins: family feuds, clashes between football fans, wars that last for generations, and also social media fights nobody remembers starting.

The Greeks knew this well. This lesson brings two of their answers to the question how do we get out of the cycle?

2. Orestes: from revenge to a court

⚖️
Revenge and justice

A cursed family

Do you remember Tantalus from Lesson 8, the guest who betrayed the gods? His descendants, the House of Atreus, betrayed and killed one another for generations. Every wrong demanded a new wrong in return. Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, was one of them. When the Greek army set out for Troy, the winds would not blow, and a seer said they would rise only if Agamemnon sacrificed his daughter Iphigenia. Agamemnon did it.

Mother and son

Ten years later Agamemnon came home from the war a victor. His wife Clytemnestra had not forgotten her daughter. She welcomed him kindly, and then she killed him. With him died the captive Trojan prophetess Cassandra from Lesson 10, who had, of course, foreseen it. Agamemnon's son Orestes was a boy at the time and grew up far from home. When he was grown, the god Apollo ordered him to avenge his father. Orestes returned and killed his own mother.

The Furies

Then the Erinyes, or Furies, appeared: ancient goddesses of vengeance who hunt anyone who sheds the blood of their own family. They did not ask for reasons. Their rule was simple: blood demands blood. They chased Orestes from city to city and drove him to madness. Orestes fled to Athens and begged the goddess Athena for help.

The first court

Athena said something new: this case is too heavy for one person to judge, not even a goddess. She chose the best citizens of Athens and founded a court on the hill of the Areopagus. The Furies were the prosecutors, Apollo the defence, and Orestes admitted the deed. The citizens voted and the votes were tied. Athena's vote decided, and Orestes was acquitted.

The Eumenides

The Furies were furious and threatened to poison the land of Athens. Athena did not drive them out. She offered them honour and a home in the city, in a cave beneath the Areopagus. The Furies accepted and became the Eumenides, the "Kindly Ones", protectors of the city. The story was told by the tragedian Aeschylus in the trilogy The Oresteia, staged in Athens in 458 BC.

Look at what really happened. The crime did not disappear: Orestes killed his mother and admits it. But for the first time the crime is not judged by the person who was harmed, but by a third party, in public, after both sides have been heard. This is the moment revenge becomes justice.

Concept

Revenge and punishment

Both do harm to someone because they did harm first. But they are not the same. Revenge is personal: the injured party carries it out, anger sets its size, and it doesn't know where to stop. Punishment is public: an impartial third party imposes it, according to rules known in advance, and it is proportionate to the deed.

Does it ask for reasonsNo: blood demands bloodYes: it hears both sides
MeasureAs much as anger demandsProportionate to the deed, by known rules
AimThat the wrongdoer suffersTo right a wrong and protect the community
EndingNone: every answer demands a new answerThe verdict closes the case
Words that stayed

The Areopagus was a real Athenian court that tried murder cases for centuries. Lawyers still speak of Athena's vote (Latin calculus Minervae): when a court's votes are tied, the decision goes in favour of the accused. The Roman principle nemo iudex in causa sua grows from the same idea: no one may be a judge in their own case. The Furies were exactly that: prosecutor, judge and executioner in one.

"An eye for an eye": revenge or a limit on revenge?

"An eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth" sounds today like a call for revenge. But when it appeared in ancient laws such as the Code of Hammurabi (around 1750 BC), it meant something different: no more than an eye for an eye. You may not kill a whole family for one lost eye. It was an early attempt to put a limit on revenge. From this idea grew the principle of proportionality: a punishment must match the seriousness of the deed.

Was the verdict on Orestes fair?

Read Aeschylus carefully and you will see the first court was not perfect. At the trial Apollo argued that the mother is not the real parent of a child, only the father, and Athena admitted she was voting for Orestes partly because she herself had no mother (she was born from the head of Zeus). Today such arguments sound unfair to Clytemnestra. The lesson is not that a court is always right. The lesson is that even an imperfect court is better than endless revenge, because its mistakes can be seen, debated and corrected.

3. Priam and Achilles: the hands that killed his son

🕯️
Mercy and shared humanity

Rage

You know Achilles from Lesson 9: the greatest Greek hero, who chose a short and glorious life. At Troy, the Trojan prince Hector killed his best friend, Patroclus, in battle. Achilles went mad with grief. He returned to the fighting, killed Hector, then tied the body to his chariot and dragged it through the dust for days. He refused to give it back to the family for burial. For the Greeks this was terrible: the dead without a funeral could find no peace.

A journey by night

Hector's father, the old Trojan king Priam, decided to do the unthinkable. He loaded a wagon with gifts to ransom his son and set out at night, almost alone, through the Greek camp. The god Hermes led him unseen to Achilles' tent.

In the tent

Priam entered, knelt before Achilles and kissed his hands, the hands that had killed his son. He said: "Remember your father, Achilles. He is old like me and waits for a son who may never come home. I have done what no father in the world has done: I have kissed the hand of the man who killed my child."

Tears

Achilles thought of his own father, Peleus, whom he would never see again, because he knew he himself would soon die. And the two of them wept side by side: Priam for Hector, Achilles for his father and for Patroclus. Enemies, and yet both just people in mourning.

Truce

Achilles gave back Hector's body. He had it washed and covered away from Priam's eyes, because he feared that at the sight Priam might lash out and he himself might lose control again. They ate together and Achilles promised eleven days of truce for Hector's funeral. Homer's Iliad, an epic that began with the word "rage", ends with this scene and Hector's funeral.

The war did not end. On the twelfth day the fighting resumes, and both Achilles and Troy will soon fall. But for one night, in one tent, the cycle of revenge stopped. No court stopped it, but two men who recognised a human being in their enemy.

⚡

Reading 1: the gods

Zeus sent word to Achilles to return the body. Achilles simply obeyed. Perhaps without an order from the sky there would have been no mercy.

👪

Reading 2: the father

Achilles saw his own father in Priam. His enemy's grief became understandable only when he recognised it as his own. That is empathy.

🤝

Reading 3: courage

The first step was taken not by Achilles but by Priam. It took more courage to enter that tent than to go onto the battlefield. Mercy often begins with the weaker one.

Concept

Forgiveness and mercy

Forgiveness is the decision to give up your right to revenge and to stop reducing the person who wronged you to that wrong alone. Mercy is when someone with the power to punish or hurt decides not to, or to do less than they could.

Careful, forgiveness is not: forgetting (Priam will never forget Hector), excusing (what Achilles did is still wrong), or giving up on justice (you can forgive someone and still want a court to decide on what they did).

Priams today: restorative justice

After the end of apartheid, South Africa set up the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996), led by Archbishop Desmond Tutu. Perpetrators publicly admitted their crimes before victims and their families, and victims could tell their stories. Today this approach is called restorative justice: the aim is not only to punish but to repair harm and rebuild relationships. Notice that Athena did something similar: she did not banish the Furies but gave them a place in the city. A victim's anger does not vanish, but it gets a place where it no longer destroys.

ConceptJustice, proportionality, impartialityForgiveness, mercy, shared humanity

We need both. Without courts, every injustice would be settled the Furies' way. But no court can make anyone recognise a human being in another. Only someone who, like Priam, has the courage to take the first step can do that.

The Furies knew only one rule: blood demands blood. Athena gave that rule a court, and Priam gave it tears. Justice stops revenge from the outside, mercy from the inside.
