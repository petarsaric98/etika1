Lesson 10

Different

A robber who cuts or stretches everyone to fit his bed. A weaver who showed the sins of the gods on her tapestry. A prophetess nobody believes. Three myths about how we treat those who don't fit our mould, and the truths we don't want to hear.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the three myths; explain the phrase Procrustean bed; tell tolerance apart from indifference and explain the paradox of tolerance; discuss whether Arachne was arrogant or brave; and use Cassandra to explain why not believing is also a moral choice.

1. One measure for everyone

Every community has its rules about what is "normal": how to dress, how to speak, what to think. That's partly a good thing, because without shared rules we couldn't live together. But what happens to those who are different? And to those who say something nobody wants to hear?

2. Procrustes: a bed made to measure

🛏️
Forcing one measure

The hospitable robber

By the road to Athens lived Procrustes. He offered tired travellers dinner and a bed for the night. He had, he said, a special bed that fitted everyone perfectly.

The bed

Only once the guest lay down did he discover the truth. If he was too short for the bed, Procrustes stretched him until he was long enough. If he was too tall, he cut off his legs. In some versions Procrustes had two beds, a short one and a long one, and always gave the guest the wrong one. So nobody ever fitted.

Theseus

On his way to Athens the young hero Theseus came by. He accepted the dinner, but didn't lie down on the bed. Instead he laid Procrustes himself on it and did to him what he had done to others.

Words that stuck

When someone forces people, ideas or facts into a fixed frame, we say they're putting them on a Procrustean bed. For example: when all students are measured by a single test and nothing else they know is seen. Or when someone "adjusts" the facts to fit a theory they already believe.

Key term

Tolerance

From the Latin tolerare, to bear. Tolerance is the willingness to accept that other people think, believe and live differently from you, even when you disagree with them. Procrustes is its opposite: he can't bear anyone being different from his bed.

Careful: tolerance is not the same as indifference. An indifferent person doesn't care. A tolerant person may disagree, but still leaves the other person room.

The paradox of tolerance: must we tolerate Procrustes?

The philosopher Karl Popper (1945) pointed out a trap: if we are unlimitedly tolerant even of those who want to destroy tolerance, they will eventually destroy it. So tolerance has a limit. Theseus didn't "tolerate" Procrustes, and he was right not to. But that raises a hard question: who decides where the limit lies without becoming a Procrustes themselves?

Your bed

A new student joins your class. He dresses completely differently from everyone else, listens to music nobody has heard of and isn't interested in what everyone talks about. Some people are already laughing.

3. Arachne: the tapestry that told the truth

🕷️
Free expression and power

The best weaver

Arachne was a young woman from Lydia, the daughter of a poor dyer. She wove so beautifully that nymphs came to watch her. People said she must have been taught by Athena herself, goddess of wisdom and weaving. Arachne was offended: nobody had taught her, she had done it all herself. She said she would happily compete even with Athena.

The contest

Athena came to her disguised as an old woman and warned her to humble herself before the gods. Arachne refused. The goddess revealed herself and the contest began. Athena wove the glory of the gods and, in the corners, scenes of mortals the gods had punished for challenging them. Arachne wove something else: how the gods, above all Zeus, deceived and carried off mortal women, disguised as a bull, a swan or a shower of gold.

The punishment

Arachne's tapestry was perfect. Not even Athena could find a single flaw in it. Furious, she tore it apart and struck Arachne. Arachne couldn't bear the humiliation and tried to hang herself. Athena didn't let her die, but turned her into a spider, to hang and weave forever. That's why spiders are called arachnids and fear of spiders is arachnophobia. The story comes from Ovid's Metamorphoses.

For centuries this myth was read as a warning against hubris, arrogance towards the gods (remember Icarus from Lesson 5). But look more closely: why did Athena punish Arachne? Not because she wove worse. Because she wove better, and because she showed the truth about the powerful.

👑

Reading 1: arrogant

Arachne was neither grateful nor modest. She boasted, challenged a goddess and insulted the gods. Talent is no excuse for arrogance.

📣

Reading 2: brave

Arachne was the only one to say out loud what everyone knew: the gods abuse their power. She was punished for being right. It's a story about freedom of expression.

⚖️

Reading 3: both

Someone can be both arrogant and right. The question is: may the powerful punish the truth because they don't like the person telling it?

Your tapestry

Your friend has drawn a brilliant cartoon of the head teacher mocking an unfair school rule. He wants to post it on the school's social media page.

4. Cassandra: a truth nobody believes

👁️
Truth and credibility

A gift and a curse

Cassandra was the daughter of King Priam of Troy and the sister of Paris. The god Apollo fell in love with her and gave her the gift of prophecy, hoping she would love him back. Cassandra refused. Apollo couldn't take the gift back, so he cursed her: she would always tell the truth about the future, but nobody would believe her.

Warnings

When Paris brought Helen home, Cassandra cried out that Troy would burn because of her. They laughed at her. When the Greeks left a huge wooden horse at the gates, Cassandra warned that there were soldiers inside. They declared her mad and pulled the horse into the city.

The fall of Troy

That night the Greeks climbed out of the horse and opened the gates. Troy was burned. Everything Cassandra had said was true, and it didn't help her in the slightest.

Video not working? Open it on YouTube.

Two ethical lessons

"No" is an answer

Cassandra was punished for refusing a god. Apollo thought his gift gave him a right to her. Told today, the myth shows something the Greeks didn't stress: a gift doesn't buy a person, and refusing is not a crime. Whoever punishes someone for saying no is abusing power.

Not believing is a choice

The Trojans weren't cursed. Cassandra was. They decided for themselves not to listen, because the truth was uncomfortable and she was "strange". So listening carries its own responsibility: when we don't take someone seriously just because they're different, or younger, or because we don't like what they're saying, we are the Trojans.

Cassandras today

In 1847 the doctor Ignaz Semmelweis showed that fewer mothers fell ill after childbirth when doctors washed their hands. His colleagues laughed at him for decades. Scientists who warned about the dangers of smoking or about climate change were Cassandras for a long time. The same goes for whistleblowers, people who discover that their company or institution is doing something wrong. Cassandra teaches us to ask: do I believe someone because of what they say, or because of who they are?

Your wooden horse

A girl in your class whom everyone thinks of as "over the top" tells you she saw an older student bullying a younger one. Nobody else noticed anything.

Final tasks

Choose one

1. The Procrustean bed today. Find one example from school, sport or social media where people are "cut and stretched" to a single measure. Who is Procrustes here, and who could be Theseus?
