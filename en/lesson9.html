Lesson 9

Choice

A young man at a fork in the road. A shepherd holding an apple in front of three goddesses. A warrior who knows how he will die. Three myths about what we choose when we choose, and what our choices say about us.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the three myths; explain what a value is and why we arrange values in a hierarchy; use Paris to explain why a judge must not accept gifts (conflict of interest); and use Achilles to discuss what matters more, a long life or a life that means something.

1. Every choice is also a sacrifice

When you choose a secondary school, you don't just choose that one. You give up all the others. When you choose between training and a birthday party on Saturday, you show what you care about more. Philosophers call these things values: what we consider important and worth the effort.

Key term

Values and the hierarchy of values

A value is something we consider good and worth striving for: health, friendship, truth, fame, money, freedom. The problem is that we can't have them all at once. When two collide, we have to decide which one stands above the other. That order is called a hierarchy of values. Your choices, not your words, show what yours looks like.

2. Heracles at the crossroads: the easy road or the hard one

🛣️
Virtue and vice

A young man at a crossroads

Before all his famous labours, Heracles was just a strong young man who didn't know what to do with himself. One day he sat down in a quiet place and wondered which road to take through life. Suddenly two women came up to him.

The first woman: Vice

The first wore heavy make-up and fine clothes, and kept glancing round to see if anyone was looking at her. She rushed ahead of the other and spoke first: "Follow me and your road will be easy and pleasant. You will never know effort or war. You will only think about what to eat, what to drink and what to enjoy. Others will work and you will take the fruits." When Heracles asked her name, she said: "My friends call me Happiness, and those who hate me call me Vice."

The second woman: Virtue

The second was simply dressed and calm. She said: "I will not deceive you. The gods give people nothing good without work and effort. If you want your friends to love you, you must do them good. If you want the fruits of the earth, you must till it. My road is long and steep, but at its end there is joy that does not fade." Heracles chose the hard road. The story was invented by the sophist Prodicus and preserved by Xenophon in his Memorabilia (memories of Socrates).

Spot the trick

Vice doesn't introduce herself as vice. She introduces herself as happiness. Nothing bad ever knocks on the door with a sign saying "I'm bad for you". Just like Midas in Lesson 4: the most dangerous thing is an apparent good, something that looks like happiness but is really a trap.

Link to Aristotle (Lesson 6)

Virtue doesn't tell Heracles "be good and you'll be happy tomorrow". She tells him that goodness is practised, like strength or music. It's the same idea as Aristotle's: virtue is a habit you gain by repeating good actions. The first time it's hard. The hundredth time it's simply who you are.

Your crossroads

You have a maths test tomorrow, and your friends are inviting you to a concert that is only on tonight.

3. The Judgement of Paris: a judge who accepted a gift

🍎
Conflict of interest

The apple of discord

All the gods were invited to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis except one: Eris, goddess of strife. Offended, she threw a golden apple among the guests inscribed "To the fairest". Three goddesses wanted it at once: Hera, Athena and Aphrodite. Zeus refused to decide, so he handed the job to a mortal, the Trojan prince Paris, who was then tending flocks on Mount Ida.

Three offers

The goddesses didn't rely on their beauty. Each offered him a gift if he chose her. Hera promised him power over Asia. Athena promised him wisdom and victory in every war. Aphrodite promised him the most beautiful woman in the world, Helen.

The verdict

Paris gave the apple to Aphrodite. The problem was that Helen was already married, to Menelaus, king of Sparta, and nobody had even asked her. Paris took her to Troy. The Greeks went after her with a thousand ships. That was the start of the Trojan War, which lasted ten years and ended with the destruction of Paris's own city.

Key term

Impartiality and conflict of interest

Paris was supposed to be a judge: to decide which goddess was the most beautiful. But as soon as he accepted an offer, he was no longer judging beauty, but what he would get. When personal gain can influence a decision that should be made fairly, it's called a conflict of interest. That's why judges, teachers and doctors must not accept gifts from the people they make decisions about. A gift doesn't have to be a bribe to spoil a judgement.

Look also at what Paris chose. He was offered three great values: power, wisdom and love (or pleasure). For centuries the myth was read as a test of the hierarchy of values: what would you put first?

Your apple

If you were in Paris's place and had to choose one gift, which would you take?

4. Achilles: a short glorious life or a long quiet one

🛡️
The meaning of life

Two fates

Achilles was the son of Peleus and Thetis, the couple whose wedding produced the apple of discord. He was the greatest Greek hero at Troy. His mother, being a goddess, knew the future, and told him that two possible fates awaited him.

The choice

If he stayed at Troy and fought, he would die young, but his glory would be immortal. If he went home, he would live a long and quiet life, but everyone would forget him. In Homer's Iliad Achilles names these two options himself. He stays at Troy and dies there, from an arrow shot by Paris.

Achilles in the Underworld

But the story doesn't end there. In the Odyssey, Odysseus goes down to the Underworld and meets the shade of Achilles. He greets him as king of the dead, the most glorious of all. Achilles answers that he would rather be a hired labourer for a poor man on earth than king of all the dead. The most famous hero in Greece, after death, doubts his own choice.

Key term

Kleos (Greek: glory)

For Homer's heroes, kleos is the glory that lives on in songs after death. For them it was the only way a mortal could outlive himself. Today it might be followers, likes or a name on a certificate. The question is the same: does it matter more how you live, or how you'll be remembered?

Your fate

You are offered two futures. In the first you become famous and the whole world knows your name, but you have little time for the people you love. In the second you live quietly, nobody outside your town knows you, but you have time for family and friends.

Heracles chose the hard road and became a god. Paris chose what he wanted and lost his city. Achilles chose glory, and then wondered whether it had been worth it.
