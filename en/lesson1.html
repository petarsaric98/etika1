Lesson 1

Myths and Morality

How stories about gods, titans and rings of invisibility became the first laboratory of the human conscience, and why we still read them 2,500 years later.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: distinguish a myth from a philosophical argument; recognise a moral dilemma in a story; explain the terms hubris, nemesis, sophrosyne and the Euthyphro dilemma; and state your own position with at least one reason.

1. Why myth at all?

Long before anyone wrote a sentence beginning “ethics is the discipline that...”, people told stories. Myth wasn't a children's fairy tale but the way a community thought out loud about things too frightening or too big for ordinary conversation: death, guilt, justice, fate, the limits of power.

Greek myth has one unusual feature that makes it perfect for an ethics class: it almost never has a clean victory. The hero who does the right thing still falls. The gods are powerful, but not always good. Punishment comes even to those who thought they were doing good. That very discomfort is the reason myth is not propaganda but a thought experiment.

Key difference

Myth answers with a story: “This is how it was, and this is what happened.”
Philosophy answers with a reason: “This is how it should be, and here's why.”

Greece is the one place in history where we can follow, almost live, the transition from the first to the second: from Homer (8th century BC) to Socrates and Plato (5th–4th century BC). It is often called the move “from mythos to logos”.

2. Five myths, five moral questions

Each of the following myths contains a question that is still completely open today. Read them as dilemmas, not as material to memorise.

🔥
Disobedience

Prometheus — the one who stole fire

The titan Prometheus (“the one who thinks ahead”) saw humans living in cold and ignorance. Zeus had deliberately withheld fire from them. Prometheus stole it and brought it to humans in a hollow reed. His punishment: chained to a rock in the Caucasus, while an eagle eats his liver every day and it grows back overnight.

Prometheus knows he will be punished; he is the one who sees ahead. And he does it anyway. This isn't an act of ignorance but a consciously accepted price.

Question for the class

Is breaking the rules ever morally right? If so, how do we tell Prometheus apart from an ordinary thief? (Key words: intention, benefit to others, willingness to accept punishment.)

Connection to today's world

Gandhi, Rosa Parks, protesters, whistleblowers who reveal that a company is poisoning a river: they all broke the law and all accepted the consequences. Philosophers call this civil disobedience. Its classic rule is exactly Promethean: you break the law publicly, non-violently and ready to accept punishment, because that shows you aren't running from justice but seeking it.

🏹
Responsibility

Pandora's box — the price of curiosity

Zeus's revenge on humanity wasn't a thunderbolt but a gift: Pandora, and with her a jar she must not open. She opened it. Out flew disease, old age, toil and envy. When she put the lid back, only one thing remained inside: hope (elpis).

People still argue about whether hope stayed as a comfort kept safe for us, or as the last evil, the one that makes us keep on suffering.

Question for the class

Are we responsible for consequences we couldn't foresee? Compare: the scientist who discovers nuclear fission and the soldier who drops the bomb.

⚖️
Conflict of duties

Antigone — when both sides are right

In Sophocles' tragedy (441 BC), Antigone's two brothers die fighting against each other. The new ruler, Creon, decrees: Eteocles, the city's defender, receives an honourable burial; Polynices, who attacked the city, is to remain unburied, food for the birds. Whoever buries him will die.

Antigone buries him. Before Creon she denies nothing. Her argument: there are unwritten, eternal laws (of the gods, of family, of humanity) that are older and stronger than the command of one man. Creon's argument: without obedience to the state the city collapses, and a ruler who gives in to a girl is no longer a ruler.

Sophocles gives no winner. Creon finally gives in, too late: Antigone, his son and his wife are dead. Tragedy is, technically speaking, a conflict in which both rights lead to ruin.

Take a side

If you were in Antigone's place, what would you do?

A short glossary of tragedy

Hamartia: a tragic error, often an oversight rather than wickedness.
Hubris: arrogance, overstepping the limit that belongs to a human.
Nemesis: the world's necessary response to hubris; the restoring of balance.
Catharsis: the purification of the audience through pity and fear.

Creon's hamartia isn't cruelty but being certain that he's right. That's the most dangerous kind of error, and it's why Antigone is still performed today.

💍
Why be good at all?

The Ring of Gyges — antiquity's most famous thought experiment

In the Republic (Book 2), Plato retells the story of the shepherd Gyges. After an earthquake he finds a golden ring in a chasm. By chance he discovers that if he turns the stone towards his palm, he becomes invisible. What does he do? He seduces the queen, kills the king and takes the throne.

Plato's brother Glaucon poses the challenge at the heart of all ethics: if nobody can see, there's no punishment and no shame, so why would anyone stay just? Glaucon claims: nobody would. Justice, he says, is merely a contract among the weak, who fear that others will rob them first.

Decide before you read on

You have a ring of invisibility. Nobody will ever find out what you did.

Plato's answer

Plato answers unexpectedly: an unjust person is punished by the very fact of being unjust. The soul has parts (reason, spirit, appetites), and justice is their harmony; injustice is inner disorder, like an illness of the body, only of the soul. That's why, he says, it is better to suffer injustice than to commit it. It's one of the most radical claims in the history of philosophy, and people still debate it.

The Ring of Gyges in the 21st century

Anonymous comments, fake profiles, copying from the internet, “when nobody's watching” at a self-checkout. The internet is a Ring of Gyges handed out to everyone. The question hasn't changed, it has just become everyday: who are you when nobody is watching?

🪂
Measure

Icarus and Sisyphus — two kinds of overstepping

Icarus was given perfect instructions: not too low (the sea will soak the wings), not too high (the sun will melt the wax). He ignored both because he got carried away. The Greeks called this hubris: overstepping the limit that belongs to a human. Its opposite is sophrosyne: self-control, prudence, a sense of measure.

Sisyphus cheated death, so his punishment was to roll a boulder up a hill and, every time, watch it roll back down. The punishment isn't pain but meaninglessness.

One must imagine Sisyphus happy.
Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus (1942)

Camus's twist: if the world has no given meaning, meaning isn't something you find but something you create, and the struggle towards the summit itself is enough to fill a person's heart. A 2,500-year-old myth became an answer to a 20th-century question.

On the temple at Delphi

Two sentences were inscribed that the Greeks considered a summary of all wisdom: “Know thyself” (gnothi seauton) and “Nothing in excess” (meden agan). The first became the programme of Socrates' philosophy, the second the foundation of Aristotle's virtue ethics: the idea that virtue is the mean between two extremes (courage between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between stinginess and extravagance).

3. The moment myth becomes ethics: Socrates

Socrates (469–399 BC) never wrote a single line. He walked the Athenian marketplace asking questions, especially of people who were sure they knew what piety, courage or justice were. His method is called maieutics (midwifery): he doesn't put knowledge into the other person, but helps them give birth to (or expose) their own view.

The Euthyphro dilemma

One question that toppled myth as the source of morality

In Plato's dialogue Euthyphro, Socrates questions a young man who is sure he knows what piety is. After several attempts, he asks a question that has never been closed since:

Is something good because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is good?

If the first is true, goodness is arbitrary: had the gods commanded torture, torture would be good. Morality becomes simply a matter of power.

If the second is true, goodness exists independently of the gods, and then we can examine it with reason. And right there, in that second answer, ethics is born as a discipline.

That's why we don't study this lesson for the stories about gods. We study it for the moment when someone first said: “Fine, but why?”, and was sentenced to death for it.

The unexamined life is not worth living.
Socrates, according to Plato's Apology

Final tasks

Choose one

1. Writing (10 sentences). “My Ring of Gyges”: describe a situation in which you could have done something with no consequences at all. What did you do, and how did you decide?

2. Debate. The class splits: team Antigone vs. team Creon. Each team must present three arguments and anticipate one counter-argument from the other side.
