Lesson 4

Good and Evil

Where does evil come from, and who is responsible for it? And what if what we want isn't what is good for us at all? Pandora and Midas: two people who got exactly what they asked for.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the myths of Pandora and Midas; distinguish evil that happens to us from evil we do; explain why Pandora's guilt is ethically disputed; distinguish the desired from the good; and state Socrates' claim that no one does evil willingly, together with one objection to it.

1. Where does evil come from?

“Good” and “evil” are the most common words in any moral conversation and at the same time the murkiest. We will easily agree that torturing a child is evil and rescuing a drowning person is good. But the moment we ask what makes something evil and where evil comes from at all, everything gets complicated.

Myths were the first attempt at an answer. If gods rule the world, why is there illness, famine and betrayal in it? The Greeks answered with a story about one jar and one woman. And then, with a second story, they added something even more uncomfortable: that evil sometimes comes out of our own desire.

🌧️

Evil that happens to us

Illness, earthquakes, the death of someone close. Nobody wanted it and nobody is to blame. Philosophers call this physical evil. It is misfortune, not guilt.

🙈

Evil that we do

Lying, betrayal, violence, silence when you should speak. That is moral evil and it has a perpetrator. Only that can be the subject of ethics.

2. Pandora: evil enters the world

This story picks up exactly where the last lesson left off — at Prometheus' theft of fire.

🎻
The origin of evil

Zeus takes revenge

Zeus punished Prometheus with chains and an eagle. But humans had the fire anyway, and that gave him no peace. So he decided to punish them too — not with a thunderbolt, but with a gift.

The making of Pandora

Hephaestus shaped the first woman out of clay and water. Every god gave her one gift: Athena the craft of weaving, Aphrodite beauty, Hermes persuasive speech and cunning, and along with it curiosity. That is why she is called Pandora — “all-gifted”, the one who received every gift.

The jar

Zeus sent her to Prometheus' brother Epimetheus, whose name means “the one who thinks afterwards”. Prometheus had warned him never to accept a gift from Zeus, but Epimetheus was enchanted and took her in. With her came a large sealed jar that must never be opened.

Day after day Pandora looked at that jar. One day she lifted the lid. Out flew every misfortune: disease, old age, hunger, envy, war, toil, death. They scattered across the whole world and could never be caught again. Pandora slammed the jar shut in terror, and at the bottom only one thing remained: hope.

It was never a box

In Hesiod, the oldest source (Works and Days, 8th century BC), the word is pithos — a big earthenware jar used to store wine and grain. The Renaissance scholar Erasmus of Rotterdam, translating it, swapped it for pyxis, “little box”. The mistake was so appealing that it has stuck ever since: everyone says “Pandora's box”, and there never was one.

Is hope a comfort or a trap?

Hesiod never says clearly what hope is doing in there, and people have been arguing about it for 2,800 years. First reading: hope was left for us — the one good thing in a world full of evils. Second reading: hope stayed locked in, so it is out of human reach. Third, and darkest: hope was in the jar because it too is one of the evils — the thing that keeps us enduring the unendurable instead of changing it.

Who is to blame here?

The myth is usually retold as “a woman's curiosity brought evil into the world” — a story much like the one about Eve. But if we read it carefully, guilt becomes a serious problem.

🧨

Pandora is guilty

She was given a clear warning and one single rule. She broke it of her own accord. Nobody held her hand.

🎪

Pandora is not guilty

She was created precisely in order to open the jar. The curiosity that drove her was not her decision but Hermes' gift. Zeus planned all of it. She is the weapon, not the perpetrator.

This is not just a question about a myth. It is the question that comes up in every courtroom and every classroom: how responsible is anyone for what has been “built into” them — by upbringing, character, circumstances, by everything they did not choose?

Your jar

You work in a lab and discover a procedure that can cure a severe disease, but the same procedure can be used to build a dangerous weapon. Nobody but you knows this yet.

3. Midas: when you get exactly what you wanted

💰
Greed and apparent good

One wish

Midas was king of Phrygia, rich and famous for never having enough. One day he found old Silenus, Dionysus' tutor, drunk and lost in his garden. He took him in and returned him to the god. A grateful Dionysus offered him any wish at all.

Midas did not think long: let everything I touch turn to gold. Dionysus looked at him and granted it.

The happiest day

Midas touched a branch — gold. A stone — gold. An apple, a table, a door, his own clothes — all gold. He was the richest man who had ever lived.

Dinner

Then he sat down to eat. The bread hardened in his hand into a gold bar. The wine turned to molten metal in his mouth. He could not eat a single bite or drink a single sip. He could not embrace anyone. Surrounded by immeasurable wealth, he began to starve.

He begged Dionysus to take the gift back. The god told him to wash in the river Pactolus. He washed the gift off himself, and from then on the river carried golden sand. The best-known version is in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

The daughter Ovid never wrote

In the best-known modern version, Midas accidentally touches his own daughter and turns her into a golden statue. That part is not ancient — it was added by the American writer Nathaniel Hawthorne in 1852. What is interesting is why: a story about a ruined dinner strikes us as a minor inconvenience, while a story about a child hits home. Hawthorne added to the myth whatever his century held most precious.

What Midas actually shows

Midas' misfortune is not that his wish went unfulfilled. It was fulfilled perfectly. The misfortune is that he wanted something that was not good for him — and he did not know it.

Concept

Apparent and real good

An apparent good is whatever seems good to us in the moment because we want it: gold, winning an argument, one more hour on the phone.

A real good is what actually makes us better and more content, and it is often duller than the apparent one. The whole of ethics, from Socrates onwards, is an attempt to learn to tell the two apart. Because “I want it” is not an argument that something is good.

Socrates' provocation

Socrates claimed that no one does evil willingly: when we do evil, we have in fact been deceived and taken an apparent good for a real one. Evil is therefore ignorance. It sounds elegant — and there is an obvious objection: we all know of people who knew exactly that they were doing wrong and did it anyway. The Greeks called that gap between knowing and doing weakness of will, and we will meet it in Lesson 7.

Dionysus makes you the offer

One wish, granted literally, no take-backs. What do you choose?

Bonus: Midas' ears (the other, less famous story)

Midas once judged a music contest between the god Apollo and Pan. He ruled in Pan's favour, and the offended Apollo gave him donkey's ears. Midas hid them under a cap, and only his barber knew the secret. Unable to bear it, the barber dug a hole, whispered into it “Midas has donkey's ears” and filled it in. On that spot grew reeds that whispered the secret to the whole world on every breeze.

The message is unnervingly modern: a secret someone knows is not a secret. Trying to bury the truth usually only decides how it will come out.

4. Comparison: two faces of evil

Both myths are about evil, but from two completely different sides: one asks where evil comes from, the other how we arrange it for ourselves.
