Lesson 12

To Believe, Not to Believe, Not to Know

In every classroom there are people who believe in God, people who don't, and people who simply aren't sure. This lesson doesn't decide who is right. It asks something else: how to understand this well and how to live together while respecting one another.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: explain what religion is and name its basic elements; tell apart theism, atheism, agnosticism and non-religiousness; explain the difference between belief and knowledge; retell the Euthyphro dilemma; state the golden rule in at least two traditions and give one objection to it; and explain what a secular state is and how it differs from an atheist one.

1. Questions every person asks

Where does all this come from? Does my life have meaning? What happens after death? Why should I be good? Neither maths nor physics answers these questions, yet people have been asking them for as long as people have existed. Some find an answer in religion, some in philosophy and science, and some say they don't know. Remember the myths we read: they too were attempts to answer these same questions.

Before we begin

In this lesson we won't grade anyone's beliefs. Ethics doesn't assess what you think, but how you justify it and how you treat people who think differently. The rule for this class: we argue about ideas, and we respect people.

2. What is religion?

The word religion comes from Latin. The Roman writer Cicero linked it to the verb relegere, "to read again, to consider carefully", while the Christian writer Lactantius linked it to religare, "to bind again": to bind people to God and to one another. Both ideas are useful.

Definition

Religion

Religion is a system of beliefs, rituals and moral rules that connects a person with the sacred, with what they consider supernatural, divine or highest, and that gathers people into a community of believers. The sociologist Émile Durkheim (1912) especially stressed that second part: religion is not only a personal conviction but also something that binds people together.

💡

Beliefs

What a religion teaches about God or gods, the world, human beings and the meaning of life. For example, belief in one God (monotheism) or in many gods (polytheism), like the ancient Greeks.

🕯️

Rituals

Prayer, holidays, fasting, pilgrimage, baptism, weddings, funerals. Rituals give shape to life's most important moments: birth, growing up, marriage and death.

📜

Moral rules

Every religion teaches how to live: the Ten Commandments, the Five Pillars of Islam, the Buddhist Eightfold Path. Many of these rules are similar across different religions.

👥

Community

Parish, mosque congregation, synagogue, sangha. Religion isn't lived only alone but with others, and that gives people belonging and support.

The largest world religions are Christianity, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism, while Judaism, though much smaller, is one of the oldest, and both Christianity and Islam grew out of it. In Croatia the great majority of people identify as Catholic, and there are also Orthodox Christians, Muslims, Protestants, Jews, members of other faiths, and people who are not religious.

Why do people have religion at all? Four answers

A believer will say: because God exists and people seek him, as a thirsty person seeks water. Saint Augustine wrote: "Our heart is restless until it rests in you."

A sociologist (Durkheim) looks at what religion does: it connects people, gives them shared values and marks the important moments of life.

A psychologist asks how religion helps the individual: it offers meaning, comfort in suffering and hope.

Critics of religion, such as the 19th-century philosopher Ludwig Feuerbach, argued that people imagined gods in their own image. Notice: the second and third answers don't decide whether God exists. They only say what religion means to people. The question of truth stays open.

3. Theist, atheist, agnostic: belief and knowledge

These words get mixed up all the time, but the difference is simple once you ask two different questions.

❓

Question 1: Do you believe?

Do you believe that God (or gods) exists? This is a question about belief. Answering "yes" makes you a theist, answering "no" an atheist.

🔍

Question 2: Can you know?

Can anyone know for certain whether God exists? This is a question about knowledge. Someone who says "I don't know, and I don't think it can be known" is an agnostic.

Concepts

Theism (Greek theos, god): the belief that God or gods exist. A believer in any religion that believes in God is a theist.

Atheism (Greek a-theos, without god): not believing that God or gods exist. Some atheists only say "I don't see enough reason to believe", while others also claim "it is certain there is no God".

Agnosticism (Greek a-gnosis, without knowledge): the view that people don't know, or can't know, whether God exists. The word was coined in 1869 by the English biologist Thomas Henry Huxley, because he didn't like any of the existing labels.

Non-religiousness: not belonging to any religion and not living by its rituals. A non-religious person isn't necessarily an atheist: some people believe in "something greater" but don't belong to any religious community. And some people are formally members of a religion but don't practise it.

 I claim to knowI don't claim to know
I believe God exists"I'm sure God exists.""I can't prove it, but I believe." (agnostic theist)
I don't believe God exists"I'm sure there is no God.""I can't prove it, but I don't believe." (agnostic atheist)

The table shows something interesting: an agnostic can be either a believer or a non-believer, because agnosticism is about knowledge, not belief. Many believers say that this is exactly what faith is: trust in what can't be proven. The Christian philosopher Blaise Pascal wrote that the heart has its reasons which reason does not know.

4. Can you be good without God?

People put this question to believers and non-believers alike. But it is more than two thousand years old, and it was first asked by our old friend Socrates.

🏛️
Plato, Euthyphro

A conversation outside the court

Socrates is waiting outside the Athenian court, because he has been accused of disrespecting the gods. There he meets a young man, Euthyphro, who has come to prosecute his own father. Euthyphro is sure he knows what piety is: whatever the gods love. Socrates asks him a question Euthyphro can't answer:

"Is something good because the gods love it, or do the gods love it because it is good?"

👈

First option

Good is good because the gods love it. But then, if the gods loved cruelty, cruelty would be good. Is goodness then just a matter of someone's will?

👉

Second option

The gods love the good because it already is good. But then goodness exists independently of the gods, and people can, at least in principle, recognise it on their own, through reason.

How do people answer today?

  • Many believers, for example the Christian philosopher Thomas Aquinas, say the dilemma is a false one: God doesn't pick the good from outside, nor invent it arbitrarily, but is goodness itself. The moral law is written into human nature, so even those who don't believe can recognise it through reason. This is called natural law.
  • Non-religious people, such as humanists, say we derive morality from reason, from compassion and from the consequences of our actions. Remember Aristotle from lesson 6: his virtue ethics describes a good life without appealing to the commands of the gods.
Worth noticing

Believers and atheists agree on one thing: there are good people among both, and neither faith nor unbelief automatically makes anyone good. The debate isn't about who is the better person, but about where morality comes from and why it binds us.

5. The golden rule: what almost everyone agrees on

Religions differ in many ways: who God is, how to pray, what happens after death. But almost all of them share one rule, known as the golden rule. Philosophers who make no appeal to religion know it too.

Definition

The golden rule

The golden rule is a moral principle of reciprocity: treat others as you would like others to treat you. It has two forms: positive ("do to others what you want them to do to you") and negative ("do not do to others what you don't want them to do to you"). The negative form is more modest: it only asks us not to cause harm. The positive one asks more: to do good.

TraditionSaying
Confucianism"What you do not wish for yourself, do not do to others." (Confucius, Analects, c. 500 BC)
Hinduism"Do not do to others what would cause you pain; this is the essence of duty." (Mahabharata)
Buddhism"Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful." (Udanavarga)
Judaism"What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour. That is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary." (Rabbi Hillel, Talmud)
Christianity"Do to others whatever you would have them do to you." (Jesus, Gospel of Matthew 7:12)
Islam"None of you truly believes until he wishes for his brother what he wishes for himself." (hadith)
Ancient Greeks"Do not do to others what angers you when done to you by others." (the orator Isocrates, 4th century BC)
Without religionHumanists derive the golden rule from empathy: the ability to put yourself in someone else's place.

Does the golden rule have a flaw? Two objections

1. Tastes differ. The writer George Bernard Shaw joked: "Do not do unto others as you would that they should do unto you. Their tastes may not be the same." You may love birthday surprises, while your friend hates them. That's why some propose the "platinum rule": treat others the way they want to be treated.

2. What if someone wants something bad? The philosopher Immanuel Kant warned that a defendant could appeal to the golden rule and tell the judge: "You wouldn't want to be convicted, so don't convict me." So the golden rule mustn't be taken literally, as granting every wish, but as a call to honestly put ourselves in the other person's place and ask what is truly good for them.

The golden rule in practice

Someone in your class group chat mocks a classmate because she goes to Mass on Sundays. A week later someone else mocks a classmate because he said he is an atheist.

6. Secularism: how people with different beliefs live together

Believers of different religions and people with no religion live in the same country. How do you organise a state so that it is fair to everyone? One answer is called secularism.

Definition

Secularism

Secularism (Latin saeculum, age, world) is the principle that the state and religious communities are separate: the state doesn't adopt any religion as official and doesn't interfere in the inner life of religious communities, and religious communities don't run the state. The state guarantees freedom of religion (the right to believe and to practise your faith publicly) and freedom from religion (the right not to believe).

Careful: a secular state is not an atheist state. An atheist state bans or persecutes religion. A secular state protects believers and non-believers equally.

Croatia

Article 40 of the Constitution of the Republic of Croatia guarantees freedom of conscience and religion and the free public expression of religion or other belief, and Article 41 states that all religious communities are equal before the law and separate from the state. The state does cooperate with religious communities, for example through treaties with the Holy See and agreements with other communities. You have an example of this in your own school: that's why you can choose between religious education and ethics. Nobody forces you into either.

Secularism or secularisation? Don't mix them up

Secularism is a principle for organising the state (separation of state and religious communities). Secularisation is a social process in which religion loses influence in a society, for example when fewer and fewer people regularly attend religious services. A state can be secular while its society is very religious, and vice versa.
