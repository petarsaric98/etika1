Lesson 2

Freedom and Fate

Two myths, two answers to the same question: if we don't choose the circumstances we live in, where does our freedom lie? Oedipus and Sisyphus through the eyes of ethics.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: explain the difference between fate and freedom; retell the myths of Oedipus and Sisyphus and identify where freedom lies in each; discuss whether a person is guilty of something they could not avoid; and explain Camus's idea that we are free even in our attitude towards what we cannot change.

1. An old question in new clothes

In the last lesson we saw that myths aren't fairy tales but the way the Greeks thought out loud. This time we return to myths with a single question, perhaps the hardest in all of ethics: are we really free?

The Greeks believed in moira: fate, the share allotted to each person, which even the gods cannot fully resist. And yet in their stories people are constantly choosing, deciding, making mistakes and answering for what they do. How do the two fit together?

🧵

Fate

What is determined in advance and doesn't depend on us: circumstances, events, the outcome. The Greeks called it moira. Today we might also say: genes, family, place of birth, chance.

🕊️

Freedom

The ability to choose for myself, so that the choice is really mine. Without freedom there is no responsibility: we don't punish the rock that fell, but the person who threw it.

Why is this an ethical question?

Praise, criticism, guilt, punishment and merit only make sense if a person could have acted otherwise. If everything is determined in advance, the words “it's your fault” and “well done” lose their meaning. That's why the question of freedom is the foundation of all ethics.

2. Oedipus: you think you're choosing, but you aren't

👁️
A fate you cannot escape

The story in brief

Oedipus's fate is pronounced in advance: he will kill his father and marry his mother. King Laius of Thebes hears this prophecy and leaves the baby on a mountainside to die. The child survives and grows up in Corinth, believing that the king and queen there are his real parents.

As a young man, Oedipus hears the same prophecy at Delphi. He freely resists it: he flees Corinth and resolves never to return. That is his free choice, and a morally good one, because he doesn't want to do evil.

But that very choice leads him straight into his fate. At a crossroads, in a quarrel, he kills an unknown old man, and that man is Laius, his real father. In Thebes he solves the riddle of the Sphinx, becomes king and marries Queen Jocasta, who is his real mother. Running away from the people he believes are his parents, he runs straight into his real father and his real mother.

When the truth comes out, Jocasta takes her own life and Oedipus blinds himself. That is how Sophocles' tragedy Oedipus the King (c. 429 BC) ends.

The message about fate and freedom

Oedipus is free to choose, but that doesn't save him. So we ask: if the outcome is determined in advance, is freedom an illusion? And is a person guilty of something they could not avoid and did without knowing?

Take a side

Is Oedipus guilty of what he did?

So why does Oedipus punish himself?

Oedipus doesn't say “I'm not guilty”. He takes responsibility for what happened at his hands, even though he didn't want it. The Greeks distinguished guilt (intention) from pollution (a deed that marks you regardless of intention). In the later play Oedipus at Colonus, the old Oedipus does defend himself: “How could I be evil by nature, when I acted without knowing?” The same hero, two answers. And that's why we still read him today.

3. Sisyphus: you can't change your fate, but you can choose how you carry it

🪨
Freedom in attitude

The story in brief

Sisyphus's fate is a punishment: he pushes a boulder uphill forever, and the boulder always rolls back down. There is no way out; he can neither escape nor stop.

Yet before his punishment, Sisyphus was an extremely free man. He was the cunning king of Corinth who tricked the gods and even chained up Death (Thanatos), so that for a while nobody on earth died. When he finally did reach the underworld, he tricked it too and returned to the land of the living.

The punishment is the gods' answer to that freedom and defiance. And it is precisely designed: not pain, but endless, futile labour.

The French philosopher Albert Camus sees something important in this. Sisyphus can't change what he does, but he can choose how he relates to it. He can despair, or he can accept his situation while fully aware that it is meaningless. That attitude is where his freedom lies.

One must imagine Sisyphus happy.
Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus (1942)
The message about fate and freedom

We don't choose our outward circumstances, but we do choose our attitude towards them. Freedom isn't always “I can do whatever I want”; sometimes it's “I can decide who I am in what I have to do”.

Your boulder

Think of something you have to do and can't change (an obligation, a family situation, an illness, school...). Which attitude is closest to yours?

4. The comparison in one sentence

Both heroes face a fate they can't change. The difference is where each of them finds freedom.

 OEDIPUSSISYPHUS
Fate A prophecy (he will kill his father and marry his mother) An eternal punishment (the boulder)
Where freedom lies In his choices, but they don't save him In his attitude towards what can't be changed
Main question “Am I guilty if I couldn't have done otherwise?” “How do I live when I can't change my circumstances?”
Oedipus teaches us that freedom doesn't guarantee the outcome. Sisyphus teaches us that the outcome doesn't take away our freedom.
