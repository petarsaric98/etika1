Lesson 7

Relating to Yourself

How much self is enough? And what do you do when you know what is right, but also know that at the decisive moment you won't be able to? Narcissus, Echo and Odysseus: three people who came face to face with themselves.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the myth of Narcissus and Echo and the episode of Odysseus and the Sirens; explain Echo and Narcissus as two vices around healthy self-respect; explain the concept of weakness of will; distinguish self-control from temperance; and name the three ways of resisting temptation that the myths offer.

1. The hardest relationship

Ethics usually talks about how we behave towards others. But the Greeks thought it all starts earlier, with how we relate to ourselves. The temple at Delphi said “Know thyself” and “Nothing in excess”. In this lesson those two rules collide.

In the last lesson we learned that virtue is a mean between two vices and is acquired by habit. Now we ask two harder questions.

🪞

How much self?

Can a person have too little self? And too much? Echo and Narcissus are two answers.

⚓

Mastery of yourself

What if you know what you should do, and also know you won't hold out? Odysseus came up with a solution.

2. Narcissus and Echo: too little and too much self

🌼
Self-love and the loss of self

A strange prophecy

When Narcissus was born, his mother asked the blind prophet Tiresias — the same one who warned Creon in Antigone — whether her son would live to old age. Tiresias answered: “He will, if he never comes to know himself.” To everyone it sounded like nonsense.

A nymph without a voice of her own

Echo was a nymph who loved to talk. While Zeus was off with other nymphs, she kept the goddess Hera busy with long conversations so she wouldn't catch him. When Hera found out, she punished her: from then on Echo could only repeat the last words someone else had spoken. She could never again speak first.

A meeting in the woods

Narcissus grew into a young man so beautiful that everyone wanted him, and he turned them all away with contempt. Echo saw him in the woods and fell in love, but she couldn't speak to him first. When Narcissus, separated from his friends, called out “Is anyone here?”, Echo answered “…here!” When he shouted “Come!”, she ran out and tried to embrace him. Narcissus tore himself away: “I would rather die than be yours!” Echo could only repeat: “…be yours.”

Ashamed, she hid in caves and wasted away until nothing was left of her but her voice. That is why we still hear her in the mountains: an echo that has no words of its own and only gives back other people's.

The pool

One of the many Narcissus had rejected prayed to the gods that Narcissus, too, would one day love something he could not have. Nemesis, goddess of retribution, heard him. Narcissus bent down to drink from a clear pool and fell in love with the face he saw in the water. He tried to kiss and embrace it, and it vanished. In the end he realised: “It's me!” Tiresias' prophecy was fulfilled — and still he could not tear himself away. He wasted away on the bank until he died, and in that spot only a flower remained, the narcissus. The best-known version is in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Self-knowledge that kills?

Socrates spent his whole life repeating “Know thyself”, yet Tiresias says Narcissus will live only if he does not know himself. How can both be true? The answer is that Narcissus did not come to know himself, only his reflection — the surface. Socratic self-knowledge means knowing your ignorance and your flaws. Narcissus' version means falling in love with your own image. The first opens a person up to others; the second shuts them inside themselves.

Aristotle's table from the last lesson has the row self-deprecation — healthy self-confidence — boastfulness. This myth might have been written for it:

🗣️

Echo: too little self

Has no voice of her own and only repeats others. She exists only through the one she loves. When he leaves, nothing of her remains.

⚖️

The mean

Healthy self-respect: having a voice of your own while still seeing the other person as a real person, not as an echo or a mirror.

🪙

Narcissus: too much self

Sees only himself. Others serve as his audience, or he doesn't notice them at all. In the end he is left alone with his reflection.

The words that stayed

This myth gave us two words. An echo is a voice without a body. Narcissism became a psychological term for excessive self-absorption at the end of the 19th century, and Freud made it famous. People talk about Narcissus a lot today. Echo may be even more common, we just notice her less: she is anyone in a group who only repeats what others think.

Your voice

In your class group chat, someone posts a nasty joke about a classmate who isn't in the group. Everyone reacts with laughing emojis. You don't find it funny.

3. Odysseus and the Sirens: knowing you won't be able to

⛵
Weakness of will and self-control

The warning

On his long journey home from the Trojan War, Odysseus stayed for a while with the sorceress Circe. When he left, she warned him about the Sirens. They sit in a meadow by the sea and sing so that every sailor who hears them turns his ship towards them and never gets home. Around them lie heaps of bones. Circe also told him how to get past them.

Wax and rope

Odysseus stopped his sailors' ears with wax so they couldn't hear a thing. He himself wanted to hear the song, so he had himself tied to the mast and gave them one order: if I beg you or command you to untie me, tie me even tighter.

The song

The Sirens did not promise mere pleasure. They promised knowledge: “Come, famous Odysseus… we know everything that happened at Troy and everything that comes to pass on earth.” Odysseus, the most curious of all the Greek heroes, longed for it with all his heart. He signalled to his sailors with his head and eyes to untie him.

Two sailors got up and tied him even tighter. The ship got past. Only when the song could no longer be heard did the sailors take the wax out of their ears and untie him. Odysseus is the only man who heard the Sirens and lived. The story comes from Homer's Odyssey, Book 12.

The Sirens weren't mermaids

For the Greeks the Sirens were bird-women, not girls with fish tails. They only got the fish tail in the Middle Ages. What matters more is what they offered: they didn't offer Odysseus beauty, but answers to every question. The most dangerous temptation is the one that targets precisely what is best in you — for Odysseus that was curiosity, just as it was for Pandora.

Concept

Weakness of will (Greek akrasia)

Weakness of will means knowing what is better and still doing what is worse. In the fourth lesson Socrates claimed this was impossible: whoever knows what is good does it. Aristotle thought it was possible, and very common. Odysseus is a perfect example: he knew the Sirens meant death, and the moment he heard them he wanted to go to them anyway. Knowledge wasn't enough. So he didn't rely on knowledge; he relied on rope.

Aristotle distinguishes four kinds of people facing the same temptation. The difference between them is not in what they know, but in what they want and what they do:

 What they wantWhat they doExample
Temperate (virtue) Doesn't want too much The right thing, without a struggle The Sirens don't tempt them
Self-controlled Wants the wrong thing The right thing, with effort Odysseus at the mast
Weak-willed Wants the wrong thing, knows it's wrong The wrong thing, then regrets it Narcissus: “It's me!” — and he doesn't leave
Self-indulgent (vice) Wants the wrong thing and thinks it's good The wrong thing, without regret Midas at the start of his story
The Ulysses contract

Psychology, economics and medicine have a term called the “Ulysses contract” (Ulysses is Odysseus' Latin name). It is a commitment you make in advance, while you are still thinking clearly, because you know that later you won't be able to resist. Examples include a savings account you can't withdraw from before a set date, a phone you hand to someone before studying, or an app that blocks social media.

Your Sirens

Greek myths know three ways of getting past the Sirens. The sailors had wax in their ears. Odysseus had rope. And when the Argonauts sailed past the same island, the singer Orpheus played louder and more beautifully than the Sirens, so nobody even listened to them.

Pick one
