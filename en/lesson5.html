Lesson 5

Measure and Virtue

So far we have asked what to do. Now we ask something else: what kind of person to become? Icarus crosses a line he could not see, and Aristotle turns that fall into a rule.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the myth of Icarus and Daedalus; explain the difference between asking “what should I do?” and “what should I be?”; define virtue and the golden mean; name both vices a given virtue stands between; explain what it means that the mean is “relative to us”; and explain why, for Aristotle, not every action has a mean.

1. Two different questions

So far we have watched people at the moment of decision: Antigone before Creon, Oedipus before the oracle, Midas before Dionysus. Each time the question was the same: what is the right thing to do in this situation?

But there is a second way of thinking about ethics. Notice that in hard moments some people behave decently almost without thinking, while others fall apart — and this has little to do with how many rules they have learned. So the Greeks asked differently: what kind of person must you be for right action to come out of you by itself?

❓

Ethics of acts

Asks: what should I do? Looks for a rule that fits the situation. It is good when a case has to be judged.

🌱

Virtue ethics

Asks: what should I be? Looks for traits of character acquired by habit. It is good when you have to raise a person, not settle one case.

This lesson takes the second road. We begin with a myth in which a single badly judged move costs a life — and in which the rule, read carefully, is already contained.

2. Icarus: the line you cannot see

🪂
Hubris and measure

Prisoners on Crete

Daedalus was the greatest inventor of his age. For King Minos on Crete he built the Labyrinth, a structure no one gets out of, to hold the Minotaur. When Daedalus helped reveal the Labyrinth's secret, Minos locked him and his son Icarus on the island. The sea was under Minos' control, and so was the land.

The wings

Daedalus collected birds' feathers and bound them with wax. He made two pairs of wings. Before take-off he gave his son one single, double warning: do not fly too high, because the sun will melt the wax, and do not fly too low, because the sea spray will weigh down the feathers. Stay close to me, keep to the middle height.

The flight

They took off and it worked. Fishermen looked up at them as if they were gods. But Icarus was a boy who for the first time in his life owned the entire sky. The thrill was louder than the warning and he began to climb — closer and closer to the sun.

The wax melted. The feathers scattered. Icarus beat his bare arms and fell into the sea that has been called the Icarian ever since. Daedalus flew on alone, and Icarus became the best-known example of hubris, of measure overstepped. The best-known version is in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

The warning had two halves

Everyone remembers “don't fly too high” and draws the lesson “don't be too ambitious”. But Daedalus also said “don't fly too low”. A low flight was no safer — it just killed more slowly and less spectacularly. So the myth does not praise humility; it praises measure: both extremes are mistakes. That is exactly the thought Aristotle will turn into a rule.

Why Icarus wasn't stupid

It is easy to say he should have listened. But Icarus had no altimeter. There was no line in the sky to tell him: too high starts here. The limit was invisible, and every metre of the climb up to the last one was perfectly safe. That is what makes the myth serious: an overstepped measure is almost never felt at the moment it is overstepped. No single cigarette, no single sleepless night and no single insult is the thing that kills.

And Daedalus?

Daedalus is no innocent victim. Before Crete, in Athens, he killed his nephew and pupil Perdix, because the boy was talented and might surpass him — he threw him off the Acropolis. That is why he was a fugitive in the first place. The man who lost a son to the boy's own excess had himself killed out of envy. The myth is far less tidy than it looks, and far more human.

How high?

You train at something you are good at. Your coach says that with twice the training you could reach an elite result, but with a real risk of permanent injury. What do you do?

3. Aristotle: virtue is a mean

What the myth of Icarus shows in a picture, Aristotle (384–322 BC) turned into a theory. His question was not “what is the rule?” but “what is a good person like?”.

Definition

Virtue (Greek arete)

Virtue is an acquired, lasting trait of character that makes a person good and makes them act well: courage, justice, honesty, generosity, gentleness.

The crucial part: virtue is not a feeling and not knowledge, but a habit. Nobody is born courageous, and nobody becomes courageous by reading a book about courage. One becomes courageous by repeating courageous acts, just as a builder becomes a builder by building. So Aristotle's answer to Socrates' “evil is ignorance” is: knowing is not enough — you have to be trained.

Definition

The golden mean (mesotes)

Every virtue stands between two vices: one that is too much and one that is too little. Courage is not the opposite of cowardice but the mean between cowardice and reckless boldness.

Daedalus' “neither too high nor too low” is that thought in a single sentence.

Two common misreadings of “the mean”

1. The mean is not mathematical. It is not “halfway” and not the same for everyone. A moderate amount of food for one person is a lot, for an athlete it is little. Aristotle says: the mean relative to us, at the right time, towards the right person, in the right way. That is why virtue requires practical wisdom, not a calculator — and why it was not enough for Icarus to know the rule by heart.

2. Not every action has a mean. There is no “moderate amount” of betrayal, malice or murder. Some things are wrong in every quantity. The mean applies to feelings and dispositions, not to all deeds — and this is the most common objection raised against Aristotle.

Concept

Eudaimonia

What is all this for? Aristotle's answer: eudaimonia. It is usually translated as “happiness”, but that is a poor translation — it does not mean a good mood, but a life that has succeeded as a whole, the life of someone who has realised the best in themselves. So nobody can be judged by a single day or a single success. People have argued about eudaimonia for centuries — not about whether we all want it, but about what it is.

One swallow does not make a spring, nor does one day; and so one act does not make a person good.
Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics (paraphrase)

Final tasks

Pick one

1. Your own table. Write down three virtues you lack and find both vices for each (too little and too much). Then, for one of them, write one concrete habit you could repeat for a week.

2. A limit with no line. Find one example from your own life where the measure is overstepped imperceptibly, step by step (studying, training, your phone, money, the way you talk about other people). Describe in five sentences how you could tell in advance that you were near the limit.

3. Icarus' diary. Write Icarus' inner monologue at the moment he decides to climb higher — but without making him stupid. Show why, in that moment, it looked like a good idea.
