Lesson 5

Measure and Limits

Where does courage end and arrogance begin? And how do you recognise a limit nobody has drawn? Icarus and Daedalus: a flight that worked — until it didn't.

Learning outcomes

After this lesson you should be able to: retell the myth of Icarus and Daedalus; explain the concept of hubris; explain why Daedalus' warning had two halves and what follows from that; explain why a limit is rarely visible at the moment it is crossed; and distinguish ambition from excess.

1. How much is too much?

On Apollo's temple at Delphi, the holiest place in the Greek world, two short rules were carved. The first was “Know thyself” — Socrates' motto from the first lesson. The second was even shorter: “Nothing in excess.”

The Greeks believed that the greatest danger to a person was not weakness but excess: the moment someone starts to believe that limits don't apply to them. We already met that mistake in the first lesson under the name hubris. Today we look at it up close, through a myth in which the limit was literal — a height in the sky.

⚠️

Hubris

Arrogance, overstepping the measure. Behaving as if rules that apply to others don't apply to you. For the Greeks it was the road to ruin — for heroes and kings alike.

⚖️

Measure

Knowing how much is enough. Not being small or timid, but hitting the right height — neither too much nor too little.

2. Icarus: the line you cannot see

🪂
Hubris and measure

Prisoners on Crete

Daedalus was the greatest inventor of his age. For King Minos on Crete he built the Labyrinth, a structure no one gets out of, to hold the Minotaur. When Daedalus helped reveal the Labyrinth's secret, Minos locked him and his son Icarus on the island. The sea was under Minos' control, and so was the land.

The wings

Daedalus collected birds' feathers and bound them with wax. He made two pairs of wings. Before take-off he gave his son one single, double warning: do not fly too high, because the sun will melt the wax, and do not fly too low, because the sea spray will weigh down the feathers. Stay close to me, keep to the middle height.

The flight

They took off and it worked. Fishermen looked up at them as if they were gods. But Icarus was a boy who for the first time in his life owned the entire sky. The thrill was louder than the warning and he began to climb — closer and closer to the sun.

The wax melted. The feathers scattered. Icarus beat his bare arms and fell into the sea that has been called the Icarian ever since. Daedalus flew on alone, and Icarus became the best-known example of hubris, of measure overstepped. The best-known version is in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

The warning had two halves

Everyone remembers “don't fly too high” and draws the lesson “don't be too ambitious”. But Daedalus also said “don't fly too low”. A low flight was no safer — it just killed more slowly and less spectacularly. So the myth does not praise humility; it praises measure: both extremes are mistakes. Aristotle will build an entire theory out of that thought — more on that in the next lesson.

Why Icarus wasn't stupid

It is easy to say he should have listened. But Icarus had no altimeter. There was no line in the sky to tell him: too high starts here. The limit was invisible, and every metre of the climb up to the last one was perfectly safe. That is what makes the myth serious: an overstepped measure is almost never felt at the moment it is overstepped. No single cigarette, no single sleepless night and no single insult is, on its own, the thing that kills.

How high?

You train at something you are good at. Your coach says that with twice the training you could reach an elite result, but with a real risk of permanent injury. What do you do?

3. Who is to blame for Icarus' fall?

The myth is usually retold as the story of a disobedient son. But if we look at all the characters, the blame starts to spread.

Daedalus is no innocent victim

Before Crete, in Athens, Daedalus killed his nephew and pupil Perdix, because the boy was talented and might surpass him — he threw him off the Acropolis. That is why he was a fugitive in the first place. The man who lost a son to the boy's own excess had himself killed out of envy. The myth is far less tidy than it looks, and far more human.

Your verdict

Icarus is dead. Who bears the greatest responsibility?

The sky had no line drawn on it. That is why measure is a matter of judgement, not obedience.

Final tasks

Pick one

1. A limit with no line. Find one example from your own life where the measure is overstepped imperceptibly, step by step (studying, training, your phone, money, the way you talk about other people). Describe in five sentences how you could tell in advance that you were near the limit.

2. Icarus' diary. Write Icarus' inner monologue at the moment he decides to climb higher — but without making him stupid. Show why, in that moment, it looked like a good idea.

3. A modern Icarus. Find a real person from sport, history or the news whose rise ended in a fall. What was their “wax”? Did they fall because they flew too high, or because nobody could see the limit?
